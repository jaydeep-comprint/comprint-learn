## Worked example: s = ut + ½at²

A scooter moving at 4 m/s accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² for 5 s. How far does it go?

**Knowns**: u = 4 m/s, a = 2 m/s², t = 5 s. **Want**: s.

$s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 4 \times 5 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 5^2 = 20 + 25 =$ **45 m**

The first part ($ut$) is how far it would go at its starting velocity; the second part ($\tfrac{1}{2}at^2$) is the
extra distance gained by speeding up. Square only the time, and don't forget the ½.

**Check with the graph**: the velocity–time line goes from 4 m/s to v = 4 + 2 × 5 = 14 m/s. The area under it is
a trapezium: ½ × (4 + 14) × 5 = 45 m. Same answer.

**From rest** (u = 0) the equation becomes $s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$. A stone dropped from rest falls with
a = 10 m/s² (taking g = 10 m/s²), so in 2 s it falls ½ × 10 × 2² = **20 m**.
